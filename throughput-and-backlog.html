Throughput is not backlog or lead time

Balance a work queue, calculate required capacity and resist a misleading waiting-time shortcut.

Fictional practice packet. Use “In this example…” when explaining an answer. Every company, person and measurement below is invented. The exercise is self-contained; a calculator is optional and no code is required.

Data and definitions

Fictional Cedar Review checks submitted catalog records. At the start of Monday, 40 records are waiting. For each of the next five working days, exactly 24 new records arrive and exactly 18 records are completed. All quantities count whole records. Each record enters once, leaves once on completion, and none is canceled, duplicated or returned for rework. Arrivals are added before that day's completions.

Fictional operating assumptions

For the second week, arrivals are again 24 per working day. Added workers are assumed equally productive immediately for this simplified exercise. Separately, five sampled completed records had elapsed lead times of 1, 1, 2, 6 and 10 working days. Lead time means time from entry to completion, including waiting. No ages of currently unfinished records are supplied.

Worked answer key

Use closing backlog = opening backlog + arrivals − completions. The daily net increase is 24 − 18 = 6 records. The five closing backlogs are 46, 52, 58, 64 and 70. Across the week, 120 arrive and 90 finish: 40 + 120 − 90 = 70. A team can complete substantial work while its queue still grows; reporting completions alone would miss that imbalance.

The second week begins at 70. To finish at 25 while accepting 120 new records, completions must total 70 + 120 − 25 = 165. Across five days this requires 33 completions per working day. That is 15 more than the current 18, an 83.33% increase rounded. The arithmetic is a capacity requirement, not evidence that the existing team can achieve it.

Four workers at six records each provide 24 per day. Arrivals then equal completions, so the queue stays at 70. Five workers provide 30 per day; across five days they complete 150, ending at 70 + 120 − 150 = 40. Six workers provide 36 per day and 180 across the week, ending at 10 under the packet assumptions. Among these whole-worker options, six can reach a backlog of at most 25; five cannot. Exactly 25 would require adjusting total completions to 165.

The sampled completed-record lead times sum to 20 working days, so their mean is 20 / 5 = 4 working days. Dividing 70 by 18 instead gives approximately 3.89 working days of nominal work if no new arrivals occur and that completion rate can be sustained. The queue is growing under the actual arrival pattern, so that quotient is not an observed lead-time statistic.

Practice follow-up

Ask a partner to play a manager who says, “We finished 90 this week; why are customers waiting?” Explain the net flow before discussing effort or blame. Have them ask what one additional worker changes. Answer with the stable queue of 70, not an unsupported promise of shorter waiting times.

Then ask what evidence would improve a lead-time claim. A useful response seeks entry and completion timestamps for a defined population, plus visibility into unfinished work. The five completed records may omit long-waiting records that have not finished. State the sampling limit explicitly.

Limits of the conclusion

Real workers may need training, different records may take different effort, and arrivals may vary. The six-record productivity assumption deliberately removes those effects; it is not a staffing recommendation. The calculations also assume no rework and no capacity loss. A daily total does not describe the distribution of individual waits or promise a completion deadline. Keep backlog, throughput and elapsed lead time separate when explaining results, and never present this fictional queue as your employment experience.
