Worked answer key

Engineering appears on R1 through R6, so its participation count is six. Design appears on R3 through R7, giving five. Operations appears on R5 through R8, giving four. Their sum is 6 + 5 + 4 = 15 team-release participations, not fifteen unique releases. The union of IDs is R1 through R8: eight unique releases.

Engineering and Design overlap on R3, R4, R5 and R6: four. Engineering and Operations overlap on R5 and R6: two. Design and Operations overlap on R5, R6 and R7: three. All three overlap on R5 and R6: two. Inclusion and exclusion gives 6 + 5 + 4 − 4 − 2 − 3 + 2 = 8. The final addition restores the three-team items after the pairwise subtractions removed too much.

Exactly one team contributed to R1, R2 and R8, so that category contains three releases. Exactly two contributed to R3, R4 and R7, also three. Exactly three contributed to R5 and R6, giving two. These categories sum to 3 + 3 + 2 = 8 unique releases. Weighting by the number of participating teams instead gives 3 × 1 + 3 × 2 + 2 × 3 = 15, which checks the participation total.

The candidate's recorded participation covers R2, R4 and R5: three of eight releases, or 37.5%. Engineering participated in six of eight, or 75%. Neither fraction measures the candidate's share of effort or impact. A supported fictional description names the test cases, API change and rollback review, then credits the other contributors. It does not say the candidate personally shipped every Engineering release.

Practice follow-up

Ask a partner, “How much of the result was actually yours?” Respond by naming the logged actions and distinguishing them from shared delivery. The answer can be concrete without claiming a numerical fraction of causation. If the partner asks for effort share, explain that the register contains participation flags rather than time records.

For another challenge, ask whether assigning one-third of each shared release to each team solves the problem. That is a possible accounting convention for three-team releases, but the packet does not establish equal effort or equal impact. A convenient allocation rule should be labeled as a convention, not discovered as a fact.

Limits of the conclusion

The register cannot tell you whether a contribution was essential, how large it was or whether the release helped users. Counting releases also ignores differences in scope. Overlap is not itself an error; it becomes an error when a participation total is labeled as a unique-output total. Preserve identifiers, define the unit and explain your own role. These are fictional practice records, not credentials or achievements to adopt for an actual interview. No hiring judgment follows from getting the arithmetic right.
