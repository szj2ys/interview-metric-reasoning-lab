A mean is not a nearest-rank percentile

Calculate a small latency distribution and explain what a slow tail changes.

Fictional practice packet. Use “In this example…” when explaining an answer. Every company, person and measurement below is invented. The exercise is self-contained; a calculator is optional and no code is required.

Data and definitions

Fictional Birch Search recorded the elapsed time for twelve completed search requests in a controlled rehearsal. Times are in whole seconds, from request receipt to completed response. These are all the observations in the exercise, already sorted. They are requests, not twelve distinct people. The recording includes a slow but valid completion; no timeout was excluded.

2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 9, 30 seconds

Use the arithmetic mean: sum divided by observation count. For a nearest-rank percentile at fraction p, select sorted position ceil(p × n), counting the first observation as position one. The ceiling operation rounds upward to an integer. Separately, define the ordinary median for an even count as the average of the two central observations. These definitions are part of the packet; do not silently switch to interpolation from another software package.

Calculate before reading the key

Compute the sum, mean, ordinary median, nearest-rank 50th, 90th and 95th percentiles. State the chosen rank for each percentile. Explain why the ordinary median and nearest-rank 50th percentile need not match. Describe the distribution without presenting one statistic as a complete performance assessment.

Then calculate a clearly hypothetical sensitivity check that omits the 30-second observation. Recompute the mean and nearest-rank 90th and 95th percentiles. Decide whether that calculation justifies removing the slow request from the actual report. Keep an audit trail of what you changed and why.

Worked answer key

The twelve observations sum to 80 seconds. Their mean is 80 / 12 = 6.67 seconds to two decimal places. The central positions are six and seven, containing 4 and 5 seconds; the ordinary median is (4 + 5) / 2 = 4.5 seconds. The nearest-rank 50th percentile uses ceil(0.5 × 12) = 6, so it is 4 seconds under the stated definition. Neither result is an arithmetic error.

The nearest-rank 90th percentile uses ceil(0.9 × 12) = ceil(10.8) = 11, giving 9 seconds. The 95th uses ceil(0.95 × 12) = ceil(11.4) = 12, giving 30 seconds. In this small sample, moving between those requested percentiles jumps from the second-largest observation to the largest. Do not imply that the extra decimal precision describes a smooth underlying curve.

Omitting the slow completion leaves eleven observations totaling 50 seconds. The hypothetical mean becomes 50 / 11 = 4.55 seconds rounded. The new 90th-percentile rank is ceil(9.9) = 10, giving 7 seconds. The 95th-percentile rank is ceil(10.45) = 11, giving 9 seconds. This shows sensitivity to one observation; it does not establish that the observation is invalid.

A valid report retains the 30-second request unless a documented collection or scope rule excludes it. You can present the sensitivity calculation alongside the full result with its changed population clearly labeled. Calling the cleaned mean “the average” without mentioning the removal hides a decision that materially changes the story.

Practice follow-up

Ask a partner, “Why is your 95th percentile so much higher than your average?” Explain the observed slow tail and the rank definition. Then have them ask, “Does this mean 95% of our customers finish in 30 seconds?” The packet measures a small set of requests, with potentially repeated users; it does not establish a customer-level guarantee or a future service commitment.

For a second pass, let the partner propose using the median alone. Explain what that would communicate about the middle and what it would hide about a slow completion. You can choose a concise headline, but preserve enough distribution detail for the reader's decision.

Limits of the conclusion

This is a descriptive rehearsal, not a representative production sample. It contains no device mix, network conditions or sampling plan. A nearest-rank convention must accompany a percentile when comparing tools that may use different definitions. No confidence interval or guaranteed future tail bound follows from these twelve rows. When discussing your own work, identify the real collection window and inclusion rules rather than borrowing these fictional observations or implying that a formula validates an entire measurement process.
