Weighted averages when the work mix changes

Explain how both groups improve while the overall average gets worse.

Fictional practice packet. Use “In this example…” when explaining an answer. Every company, person and measurement below is invented. The exercise is self-contained; a calculator is optional and no code is required.

Data and definitions

Fictional Alder Help records hands-on handling time for completed support tickets. A minute means one minute of active work, excluding waiting for a customer. Each ticket belongs to exactly one category. The following group averages are exact for this exercise, not rounded estimates. Every completed ticket appears once, and the category definitions remain unchanged between weeks.

A manager says, “The overall average increased, so every team became slower.” A colleague says, “Both categories improved, so the actual overall average must have fallen.” Neither has shown a calculation. You are explaining the measurement, not deciding whether faster handling necessarily means better support.

Calculate before reading the key

Calculate each week's total handling minutes and weighted mean. Show how many tickets supply the denominator. Calculate the within-category percentage reductions and the overall percentage change. Then standardize Week B to Week A's category proportions: what average would those Week B category means produce with an 80% simple and 20% complex mix?

Write separate statements for the observed average and the standardized comparison. Do not call the hypothetical mix an actual week of work. Before opening the answer key, predict whether giving equal weight to the two categories would answer the same question as giving equal weight to tickets.

Worked answer key

Week A contains 100 tickets. Simple work totals 80 × 4 = 320 minutes; complex work totals 20 × 20 = 400 minutes. Total handling time is 720 minutes, so the observed mean is 720 / 100 = 7.2 minutes per ticket. Week B also contains 100 tickets, but its totals are 40 × 3 = 120 and 60 × 18 = 1,080 minutes. Its 1,200 total minutes give a mean of 12 minutes per ticket.

The overall mean increased by 4.8 minutes, or (12 − 7.2) / 7.2 = 66.67% rounded to two decimal places. Meanwhile the simple-category mean fell by (4 − 3) / 4 = 25%, and the complex-category mean fell by (20 − 18) / 20 = 10%. Both facts can hold because complex tickets became 60% of completed work instead of 20%.

At Week A's fixed mix, Week B's mean would be 0.8 × 3 + 0.2 × 18 = 6 minutes. Compared with Week A's 7.2, that is a 1.2-minute reduction, or 16.67%. This is a standardized descriptive comparison, not a claim that Week B actually consumed only 600 minutes. Its actual recorded total remains 1,200.

An equal average of group means gives (4 + 20) / 2 = 12 minutes for A and (3 + 18) / 2 = 10.5 for B. Those calculations assume a 50/50 category mix. They are arithmetic answers to a different question, not the observed per-ticket means. Name the weights before announcing a result.

Practice follow-up

Ask a partner to challenge: “Were you faster or slower?” A useful reply separates levels: the observed per-ticket mean rose with a more complex mix, while both category means fell. Have the partner repeat the denominator and the comparison mix. If they cannot, revise the explanation rather than adding more decimal places.

Next ask what happens if tickets move between categories after work begins. The current calculation depends on stable classification. A real analysis would inspect category rules, exclusions and unfinished work before treating these groups as comparable. These fictional numbers are practice material, never evidence of your own work history.

Limits of the conclusion

Neither comparison establishes why the category means changed. Different staff, ticket difficulty within categories or incomplete work could matter. A lower handling mean might reflect efficient work or rushed work; quality is unmeasured here. Standardization makes the chosen mix explicit but cannot repair unobserved differences. Report the observed and standardized results together, explain their different purposes, and avoid using whichever number makes your preferred story look best.
