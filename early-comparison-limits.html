An early comparison is not a settled result

Reconstruct a small comparison and identify what stopping and assignment leave unresolved.

Data and definitions

Fictional Elm Import is comparing two file-check instruction screens. A successful attempt means a submitted practice file passes every required check on that attempt. Each participant makes one attempt, so the counts here are both attempts and distinct participants. Version A is shown to a morning class; version B to an afternoon class. People are not randomly assigned, and the classes may differ in prior experience.

The written plan was to observe 50 participants per version with the same success definition. Only 40 per version have been observed. A teammate proposes stopping now because B looks better. No significance threshold, randomization procedure, device balance or preplanned stopping rule beyond the observation count is provided. The unobserved attempts have no known outcomes.

Calculate before reading the key

Calculate each block's rate and the cumulative rate for each version. Explain why averaging the two block percentages equally gives the wrong cumulative rate. State the cumulative difference in percentage points and relative to A. Keep this descriptive calculation separate from a claim that the screen caused the difference.

Determine the smallest and largest possible final difference if the planned remaining participants are observed. Use only the allowed success counts; do not assign probabilities to the extremes. Recommend what to report now and what an improved comparison plan would need.

Worked answer key

The first-block rates are 8 / 10 = 80% for A and 9 / 10 = 90% for B. The next-block rates are 18 / 30 = 60% and 21 / 30 = 70%. Cumulative A is (8 + 18) / (10 + 30) = 26 / 40 = 65%. Cumulative B is (9 + 21) / 40 = 30 / 40 = 75%. An equal average of the block rates gives 70% and 80%, incorrectly weighting ten attempts as heavily as thirty.

The observed cumulative difference is 10 percentage points. Relative to A's 65%, it is 10 / 65 = 15.38% rounded. “B improved performance by 15.38%” would imply a causal claim that the assignment does not establish. A safer statement says the observed rate in B's afternoon group was higher by those amounts in the completed observations.

Ten attempts remain for each version. A's final successes can range from 26 to 36, so its final rate can range from 52% to 72%. B can finish with 30 to 40 successes, or 60% to 80%. The smallest possible B-minus-A difference is 60% − 72% = −12 percentage points. The largest is 80% − 52% = 28 percentage points. These endpoints show that the observed direction can reverse; they are not confidence intervals or likely-outcome bounds.

Continuing to the planned count addresses the incomplete-count issue but does not remove the morning-versus-afternoon confound. More observations under the same biased assignment cannot, by themselves, identify the screen's causal effect. Stopping because a desirable result appeared also changes the reporting decision after seeing the data. Record the actual stopping decision and all observed counts rather than pretending the original plan was completed.

Practice follow-up

Ask a partner, “Should we roll out B today?” Separate a reversible product decision from the strength of the evidence. You might gather more observations, inspect usability issues or run a better-balanced comparison. If choosing a monitored rollout for practical reasons, state that it is a decision under uncertainty, not a statistically established win.

Have the partner challenge the final-rate bounds. Explain that the minimum combines all remaining successes for A with none for B, while the maximum does the reverse. These are logical possibilities permitted by the packet. Nothing here says they are equally likely, or supplies any probability at all.

Limits of the conclusion

The exercise does not supply enough design information to justify a significance test or a causal verdict. Participant background, class timing and devices may differ. A future plan should specify assignment, outcome definition, observation horizon and how stopping decisions are made before reviewing results. That is a measurement-design discussion, not a guarantee that a particular sample size suffices. Keep the original counts visible and use your own real evidence when discussing employment history.
